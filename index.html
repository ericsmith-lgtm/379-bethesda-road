The rules

The law on false sworn statements

False swearing

S.C. Code Ann. § 16-9-30

It is unlawful to wilfully and knowingly swear falsely in taking any oath required by law before a person authorized to administer it. A felony, punishable by a fine, up to five years in prison, or both.

Perjury

S.C. Code Ann. § 16-9-10

It is unlawful to wilfully give false, misleading, or incomplete testimony under oath in a court or judicial proceeding (a felony, up to five years), or wilfully give false, misleading, or incomplete information on a document or form required by state law (a misdemeanor).

Search warrants

S.C. Code Ann. §§ 17-13-140, 17-13-150

A warrant issues only on a sworn affidavit, and only for the kinds of property the statute lists: stolen or embezzled property, contraband, property used to commit a crime, evidence of crime, and certain drugs. It must be returned within ten days with a signed inventory, and the person served is to receive a copy of the warrant and the affidavit.

The Fourth Amendment

Franks v. Delaware, 438 U.S. 154 (1978)

A warrant cannot stand if the affiant included a false statement knowingly and intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth, and the statement was necessary to the finding of probable cause.

Leaving facts out

Miller v. Prince George’s County, 475 F.3d 621 (4th Cir. 2007)

The Fourth Circuit, which covers South Carolina, holds an officer liable who deliberately or with a ‘reckless disregard for the truth’ made material false statements in his affidavit, or who omitted material facts with the intent to make, or with reckless disregard of whether they thereby made, the affidavit misleading.

Civil and criminal civil-rights law

42 U.S.C. § 1983 · 18 U.S.C. § 242

Section 1983 lets a person sue an official who, under color of law, deprives him of a constitutional right. Section 242 makes it a federal crime to do so willfully.

The law

What being told does under the law

Mr. Smith’s position is that the officials he notified were obligated to act once they knew. These are the rules that make notice matter.

A county is liable for what its policymakers decide or let happen

Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)

Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 a county answers for a constitutional violation caused by its own policy or custom, including decisions of the officials who hold final policymaking authority. County Council is the County’s governing body.

Knowing and doing nothing can be “deliberate indifference”

City of Canton v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378 (1989)

A municipality is liable when its policymakers know their subordinates are depriving people of rights and respond with “deliberate indifference.” Notice is the first element.

Supervisors who are told and stay silent

Shaw v. Stroud, 13 F.3d 791 (4th Cir. 1994)

In the Fourth Circuit, which covers South Carolina, a supervisor is liable when he has “actual or constructive knowledge” of conduct that poses a pervasive and unreasonable risk of constitutional injury, his response is “so inadequate as to show deliberate indifference to or tacit authorization” of it, and there is an “affirmative causal link” to the injury.

Notice before taking property

Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950); Jones v. Flowers, 547 U.S. 220 (2006)

The Fourteenth Amendment requires notice “reasonably calculated” to reach the owner before the government takes his property, and additional steps when it learns its notice did not arrive. The County’s log records its certified letters coming back; Mr. Smith’s three letters told it exactly where he was.

Public records must be produced

S.C. Code Ann. § 30-4-30

A public body must respond to a records request within ten business days and produce within thirty days of that response. The County closed request 25-1384 on Nov. 24, 2025 without producing the asbestos assessment, the permit file or the warrant file.

  • No complaint, hearing, findings or demolition order addressed to Mr. Smith appears in the County’s production or in the 31 pages it filed to support its claim. IPMC § 110, which the liens cite, requires notice and an order before demolition.
  • No proof that the County paid the invoices, and no credit for salvage, grants or reimbursements, has been produced.

  • The County’s own sworn declaration (Doc. 104 ¶ 5) calls $40,350 of the claim “demolition costs.” Its claim calls it “property taxes.”
